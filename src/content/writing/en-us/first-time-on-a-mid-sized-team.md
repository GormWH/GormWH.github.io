---
title: "I thought I disliked rules"
description: "I started a stricter, bigger job expecting to miss the freedom of a small one — two weeks in I understood the rules were the point."
date: 2026-07-28
kind: "Essay"
tags: ["Career", "Reflection"]
---

My new job started with a list of things I could not do. Security rules put some of the tools I lean on out of reach, the schedule was tighter than anything I was used to, and there was a report due on every task. My first reaction was that I might have traded something good for something worse. Two weeks in I changed my mind, and the reason surprised me.

What changed was not that the place was bigger. It was that every rule had a reason I could see, and the reason held for everyone. I had once chosen a small firm partly for its flexibility, on the theory that fewer rules meant more room to do good work. What I missed then is that flexibility can also mean nothing is ever settled — and a rule you can argue with every morning is not really flexibility, just uncertainty wearing a friendly face.

At an earlier job I was asked to write a daily report of what I had done, mostly on the days I worked from home. The unspoken logic was that work you cannot see is work you cannot trust. But presence was never the same as progress. Sitting in an office all day told no one what I had actually built, and neither did a paragraph written to prove I had been busy. The report measured whether I could be observed, not what I made. A rule like that has no real why behind it — only someone's unease — and rules built on unease tend to land unevenly, which is the tell that you are working for a person's peace of mind rather than for the work.

The new place tracks work on a board of tickets. The deal is simple: keep your ticket current and your progress is visible to everyone by default. No one has to watch me, and no one is blamed for work that happens to be invisible on a given afternoon. The rule asks something of me, but its reason is obvious — the whole team reads the same board — so keeping it does not feel like being watched. It is what makes being watched unnecessary.

So I have stopped telling myself I dislike rules. I dislike rules whose only reason is someone's discomfort, because there is nothing in them to agree with. Give me a rule with a real why — one that applies to me and to the person who wrote it alike — and I will keep it without being asked. That, not the size of the team or the freedom to bend things, turns out to be what I had been looking for.
